Deepfakes and AI Fakes

AI can now make fake photos, videos and voices of real people from just a few pictures or seconds of audio. That means seeing and hearing is no longer believing. It also means someone could make a fake of you, or be tempted to make one of someone else. In Hong Kong, sharing or threatening to share an intimate image without consent is a crime, and that includes AI-made and edited images.

The rules

  1. Never make, request, save or forward a fake image or video of a real person, especially a sexual one, even 'as a joke'.
  2. If someone makes a fake of you: it's not your fault. Tell a parent, keep evidence, report it, and use Take It Down.
  3. A face or voice is not proof. If a call or message asks for money, codes or photos, hang up and check with the real person.
  4. Agree a family code word for emergencies.
  5. Keep your account private and limit public selfies and face videos: they're the raw material for fakes.
  6. Check celebrity offers on their official verified account. Real giveaways never ask you to pay.

Real-world deepfake examples

Deepfakes are used for three things: humiliation, blackmail and money. The defences are the same: don't make or share them, don't trust a face or voice on its own, check with the real person, and tell a parent.

The classmate's 'joke' app

A student uses a 'nudify' app on normal photos of classmates, taken from their Instagram, and shares the fakes in a group chat as a joke. The images spread to other schools within days.

Real life: In 2025, a University of Hong Kong law student was accused of using AI to make explicit fake images of about 20 women, including classmates and teachers. Hong Kong's privacy watchdog opened a criminal investigation.

Warning signs: Anyone talking about 'AI apps that undress people'; fake images being passed around 'for a laugh'.

What to do: Never make, ask for, save or forward one. Tell a teacher or parent. If it's of you: tell a parent, report it, and use Take It Down.

The video call from 'the boss'

An employee joins a video call with what looks and sounds like the company's finance chief and several colleagues. Everyone on the call is a deepfake. He's told to transfer money urgently, and he does.

Real life: This happened in Hong Kong in 2024: a company lost about HK$200 million after a deepfake video conference.

Warning signs: Urgent money requests, secrecy, people who seem slightly 'off', a call you weren't expecting.

What to do: If it's about money or codes, hang up and check with the real person on a number you already have.

'Mum, I've lost my phone'

A teen gets a voice message that sounds exactly like a parent, saying they're in trouble and need the teen to send a code or transfer money right away.

Real life: A few seconds of someone's voice from a video is enough for AI to copy it.

Warning signs: A familiar voice asking for money, passwords or codes, in a hurry, from a new number.

What to do: Hang up and call the parent on their usual number. Use the family code word.

The face-swap romance

Someone online looks perfect on video calls, builds a relationship over weeks, then needs money for an emergency or an 'investment'.

Real life: In 2024 Hong Kong Police broke up a romance-scam gang that used face-swapping on video calls to look like attractive young people.

Warning signs: Too good to be true, never meets in person, eventually money.

What to do: A video call is no longer proof someone is real. Never send money or images to someone you've only met online.

The celebrity giveaway

A video shows a famous footballer or YouTuber promising free money or prizes if you send a small 'fee' or log in through a link.

Real life: Deepfake celebrity ads and videos are a common scam on social media and video sites.

Warning signs: Celebrity plus free money plus a link or fee.

What to do: Check the celebrity's official verified account. Real giveaways never ask you to pay.
