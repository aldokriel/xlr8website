Passwords, Passkeys and Phone Locks

Your passwords and phone lock are the front door to everything: your messages, photos, accounts and sometimes money. Most account takeovers aren't clever hacking. They happen because someone reused a password, gave away a code, or let someone watch them type a PIN. Passkeys are a newer, safer way to log in that can't be phished.

The rules

  1. Use a different password for every account. Let the phone's built-in password manager remember them.
  2. Use passkeys wherever a site offers them: you log in with your face, fingerprint or PIN, and there's nothing to steal or type into a fake site.
  3. Turn on two-step verification on important accounts. An authenticator app is better than SMS.
  4. Never share passwords, codes or backup codes: not with friends, a boyfriend or girlfriend, or 'support'. Parents only.
  5. Lock your phone with Face ID or fingerprint plus a 6-digit or longer PIN (no birthdays or 123456), and a short auto-lock.
  6. Hide message previews on the lock screen, and turn on Stolen Device Protection (iPhone) or Theft Protection (Android).

Real-world examples

Each of these worked because of one small habit. Fix the habit and the attack fails.

The giant password leak

Login details leaked from one website get tried automatically on Instagram, Gmail, games and more. Anyone who used the same password everywhere loses every account at once.

Real life: In 2025 HKCERT urged Hong Kong users to check their accounts after a reported worldwide leak of about 16 billion account passwords.

Warning signs: Using the same password on more than one site.

What to do: Use a different password for every account (a password manager remembers them), turn on two-step verification, and switch to passkeys where offered.

The 'backup code' trick

A message that looks like it's from Instagram says your account will be locked unless you 'verify' by sending your backup codes. Those codes let the scammer skip two-step verification and take the account.

Real life: HKCERT has warned of phishing campaigns that target Instagram backup codes to get around two-step verification.

Warning signs: Anyone, even 'support', asking for codes, backup codes or screenshots of security settings.

What to do: Never share codes with anyone. Store backup codes privately with a parent.

The watched PIN

A thief watches someone type their phone PIN in a busy place, then steals the phone. With the PIN they can change the Apple or Google password and lock the owner out of everything.

Real life: Apple added Stolen Device Protection after reports of thieves watching people type their passcodes and then stealing their phones.

Warning signs: Typing your PIN where people can see; a short or simple PIN.

What to do: Use Face ID or fingerprint in public, cover the screen when typing your PIN, use a longer PIN, and turn on Stolen Device Protection (iPhone) or Theft Protection (Android).
