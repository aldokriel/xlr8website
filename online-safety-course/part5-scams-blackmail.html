Blackmail, Scams and Fake Accounts

Criminals target teenagers because they think teens will panic and stay silent. A common trick in Hong Kong is 'naked chat blackmail': a fake profile flirts, gets a photo or video, then demands money. In one half-year, HK Police logged 833 cases; 90% of victims were male and about one in five were students, the youngest aged 11.

The rules

  1. Fast romance from someone you've never met, plus a request for a photo, is a trick.
  2. If anyone threatens you: don't pay, don't send anything more, keep the messages, tell a parent straight away.
  3. Being blackmailed is never your fault, and your parents will help.
  4. Never click prize links or 'free followers' apps that want your login.
  5. Check anything suspicious with Scameter+ or call the HK Anti-Scam Helpline 18222.

Real-world blackmail examples

Every one of these starts small. The way out is always the same: stop, don't pay, keep the evidence, tell a parent.

The 'girl' who wanted to video chat

A teenage boy gets a follow from an attractive girl he doesn't know. She's friendly, flirts fast, moves the chat to another app and suggests a video call. During the call he's encouraged to undress. Afterwards the 'girl' turns out to be a criminal gang: they've recorded the call and send him a list of his friends and family, demanding money or they'll share it.

Real life: This is 'naked chat blackmail', Hong Kong's most common form. HK Police have reported victims as young as 11; in one case the parents of an 11-year-old were blackmailed for HK$10,000, and two 12-year-old boys were among 61 victims in a single week.

Warning signs: Stranger, fast flirting, move to another app, video call, undressing.

What to do: Never undress on camera. If it's happened: stop replying, don't pay, screenshot the threats and their profile, tell a parent, report to police.

The breakup

Two teens were dating and one sent the other a private photo. After they break up, the ex says they'll send it to the whole year group unless the other gets back together with them, or sends more.

Real life: Threatening to publish intimate images without consent is an offence in Hong Kong, even if the photo was given willingly.

Warning signs: Anyone asking for private photos 'because you trust me'; anger or control in a relationship.

What to do: Don't send more. Tell a parent or school counsellor. Keep the messages. Use Take It Down to block the image on major platforms.

The hacked account

A student's Instagram is taken over after they enter their login on a 'free followers' site. The hacker messages them: pay in gift cards or crypto, or all their private DMs get posted and the account deleted.

Real life: Account takeovers usually start with a fake login page or a shared verification code.

Warning signs: Sites promising followers, 'log in to claim', requests for the code texted to you.

What to do: Don't pay. Tell a parent, use the app's account-recovery page, change passwords everywhere, turn on two-step verification.

The fake image

Someone sends a teen an AI-made fake nude of them, built from a normal selfie, and threatens to share it unless they pay or send real photos.

Real life: Hong Kong's 2021 law covers altered and AI-made intimate images. Take It Down also works for AI images.

Warning signs: A stranger with your public photos; any threat involving an image of you.

What to do: It's not your fault and not real. Tell a parent, don't respond, keep evidence, report it to police and the platform.

The group that keeps asking for more

A teen joins an online group that feels like belonging. They're asked for a 'harmless' picture to prove loyalty. Then the picture is used to demand harder 'tasks', each recorded, each used as the next threat.

Real life: This is how the FBI describes networks like '764', linked to the Malmesbury arrest in August 2026.

Warning signs: Secret groups, loyalty tests, tasks that escalate, being told you can't leave.

What to do: Leave immediately and tell a parent, however far it has gone. The way out is telling someone.

'I'll tell your parents'

A teen did something they're embarrassed about, like breaking a rule or going somewhere they shouldn't. Someone who knows threatens to tell their parents unless the teen does what they want: hand over money, a login, or a photo.

Real life: Blackmail works by making you more afraid of being found out than of the blackmailer.

Warning signs: Anyone using a secret to make you do things.

What to do: Tell your parents yourself. That takes the blackmailer's power away. In this family, telling us never gets you in more trouble than hiding it.
